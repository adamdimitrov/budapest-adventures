Great Market Hall or Jewish Quarter?

Budapest's food tours split into two camps. Market tours (#1, #3, #5, #8, #9) start at the Great Market Hall on Fővám tér: daytime, lots of small tastings, paprika and sausage stalls. Jewish Quarter tours (#2) start in District VII, the old Jewish neighbourhood that is now the ruin bar district: more of a sit-down meal and drinks, better in the late afternoon or evening.

One thing to plan around: the Great Market Hall is closed on Sundays and closes early on Saturdays, so market tours change their route or start point. If the market is the reason you're booking, go Monday to Friday.

Best Time to Do a Food Tour in Budapest

Do it on your first or second day. You'll eat well, and you'll leave with a list of dishes and places to order from for the rest of your stay. Book a lunchtime market tour if you want the Great Market Hall, or an evening tour (#2 or #4) if you'd rather it replace dinner. Skip breakfast or eat light: every guest review on the bigger tours says the same thing about portion sizes.

Make a full day of it: pair a morning market tour with an afternoon walking tour, then finish on the river with one of the dinner cruises or a sunset prosecco cruise. In December, the Budapest Christmas markets are the other big food stop: lángos, goulash in a bread bowl and chimney cake at every stall. Staying on a budget? Several of the best hostels in Budapest sit right by the Great Market Hall. Wine lovers can go to the source: the Etyek and Páty wine villages are 20 to 30 minutes from the city, and both are in our best day trips from Budapest. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best food tour in Budapest?

The Foodapest Market to Tavern tour (3 hours, from €62) is the best all-rounder: it starts in the Great Market Hall and includes 12+ tastings such as goulash, lángos and chimney cake, plus pálinka and a wine tasting. It is rated 4.9 by 679 guests.

How much does a food tour in Budapest cost?

Established group food tours cost €53 to €62 per person for 2.5 to 3 hours. A hands-on cooking class with a market tour is €90, and a 65-minute chimney cake workshop is €20. That works out at about €20 per hour for most walking food tours.

Which Budapest food tour is the cheapest?

The cheapest proven walking food tour is Carpe Diem's Jewish Quarter tour at €53 under its street food listing. The same company sells the same 4-stop format for €62 under two other names, so check which listing you are booking.

Do Budapest food tours run on Sundays?

Many do, but the Great Market Hall is closed on Sundays, so market-based tours change their route or start point that day. If seeing the market matters to you, book Monday to Saturday. The Jewish Quarter tours do not depend on the market.

What food will I try on a Budapest food tour?

Expect goulash, lángos (deep-fried flatbread), chicken paprikash, cold cuts and pickles, chimney cake and desserts such as somlói trifle, usually with pálinka, Hungarian beer or Tokaji and other wines.

Do I need to eat dinner after a food tour?

On the Carpe Diem, Foodapest and Experiences by George tours most guests say no: the portions add up to a full meal. The chimney cake workshop is a single sweet, so plan a meal separately.

Are Budapest food tours good for vegetarians or gluten-free diets?

Vegetarians are usually catered for if you say so when booking. Gluten-free and vegan diets are harder: Carpe Diem says it can't cater for them yet. Tell the operator about any diet before you book.

Should I tip the guide on a Budapest food tour?

Tips are not included on the Foodapest or Experiences by George tours. If you enjoyed it, €5 to €10 per person in cash is a common thank-you.

Is a food tour or a cooking class better in Budapest?

A food tour covers more of the city and more dishes in less time. A cooking class is better if you want to learn a recipe and cook it at home. The Foodapest cooking class includes a market tour, so you get a bit of both.
